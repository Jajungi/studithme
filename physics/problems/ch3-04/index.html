Why is $\vec E = 0$ inside a conductor in equilibrium?

Answer
Free charges rearrange until internal E=0E=0E=0; Qenc=0Q_\mathrm{enc}=0Qenc​=0 inside
  1. Goal. Explain in words why the electrostatic field must be zero everywhere inside a conductor in equilibrium, and give one Gauss’s-law sentence supporting that conclusion.
  2. Free charges move. In a conductor, conduction electrons (or ions) are mobile. Any nonzero E⃗\vec EE would exert force and drive charge motion.
  3. Not equilibrium yet. If E⃗≠0\vec E\neq 0E=0 in the bulk, charges keep accelerating and redistributing until they cancel the internal field—so static equilibrium cannot coexist with bulk E⃗\vec EE.
  4. Where charge ends up. In equilibrium, excess charge sits on surfaces (or internal interfaces); the interior averages to neutral on macroscopic scales.
  5. Gauss’s-law line. A Gaussian surface wholly inside the metal encloses no net charge (Qenc=0Q_\mathrm{enc}=0Qenc​=0), hence ∮E⃗⋅dA⃗=0\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec A=0∮E⋅dA=0; for a conductor’s symmetry this implies E=0E=0E=0 throughout the interior.
  6. Scope. This argument is for electrostatics. Time-varying fields can penetrate metals and require displacement current in Ampère–Maxwell law.
  7. Final answer. Free charges rearrange until internal E=0E=0E=0; Qenc=0Q_\mathrm{enc}=0Qenc​=0 for any surface fully inside.
